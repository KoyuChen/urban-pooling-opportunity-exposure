\section{Introduction}

Many public datasets are row-complete but relation-incomplete. A release may
preserve every transaction's timestamp, location, duration, and outcome while
removing the key that says which rows belonged to the same referral episode,
household visit, shared ride, or service run. Analysts can still count rows,
but relational statistics---within-event dispersion, group composition,
exposure, or resource utilization---depend on an unobserved partition.
Selecting one linkage or one clustering converts release uncertainty into
analyst certainty and can make downstream estimates look much more precise than
the public data justify.

Ride pooling makes the distinction concrete. A public row can report that a
trip was successfully shared without revealing its co-riders or vehicle-run
identifier. Pairwise reconstruction is already ambiguous, but high-capacity
pooling is not pairwise. Three trips may satisfy
$A\leftrightarrow B$ and $B\leftrightarrow C$ while
$A\not\leftrightarrow C$; all three can belong to one run if passengers enter
and leave sequentially and simultaneous occupancy never exceeds capacity.
Timestamp coarsening adds another layer: the public record may reveal only a
set containing the exact interval. Treating the entire outer envelope as the
realized trip is incorrect because it can create artificial concurrency. The
right quantifier is existential: a latent exact timestamp completion must exist
inside each released support.

We ask a deliberately different question from partner recovery:
\begin{quote}
Which aggregate conclusions hold over all latent temporal-event worlds
consistent with the declared release information?
\end{quote}
The output is a lower and upper endpoint, each accompanied by a feasible-world
witness or an explicit unresolved status. The interval is conditional on the
candidate universe, timestamp supports, and capacity assumption; it is not a
confidence interval and does not imply that every interior scalar is attainable.

This paper makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Identification.} We define core--buffer ordered-run worlds with
set-valued timestamps, positive-overlap connectivity, occupancy-capacity
constraints, and aggregate query functionals. We prove joint nesting in time
support and capacity and define a capacity-equivalent information-loss index.
\item \textbf{Algorithms.} We characterize one fixed-span rooted run by
segment coverage and boundary bridge rows. The resulting consecutive-ones structure
gives an integral LP oracle for additive pricing. A Dantzig--Wolfe master uses
this oracle to certify the full LP relaxation. We also exhibit master
nonintegrality, locating the remaining difficulty in cross-run assignment.
\item \textbf{Evidence.} Chicago and NYC expose complementary release regimes.
The audits separate hidden-linkage ambiguity, time-support uncertainty, and
capacity uncertainty; they publish only aggregate endpoints and solver
certificates, not reconstructed links.
\end{enumerate}

The empirical findings are modest in scope but sharp within that scope. In a
fixed NYC pairwise benchmark, replacing exact seconds by artificial 15-minute
outer intervals increases candidate edges by 79.5\% and several identified
widths by 12--15\%. In an ordered-run common-support comparison, most of the
$C=2$ to $C=4$ widening occurs already at $C=3$. Across six predeclared audit
windows, artificial timestamp supports expand certified reachable run support
at $C=2$ and $C=3$ in every window, but the amount equivalent to a one-step
capacity relaxation varies by regime. Fine temporal resolution therefore does
not imply fine relational resolution.
